Morsel: from recipe saver to cooking companion

RoleSolo designer & developer
PlatformsAndroid, iOS in TestFlight
StatusVersion 1.0.22

Summary

Morsel is a mobile app that turns a recipe from Instagram, TikTok or a blog into a clean recipe card you can use offline. I designed and built it on my own, and it’s live on Google Play.

  • The problem: Good recipes are buried in short videos and long blog posts, so people end up with screenshots and saved links they can’t search or cook from.
  • What I did: Designed and built the whole product, including the app and the server behind it. In three months I grew it from a recipe saver into a cooking companion with meal planning, grocery lists and calorie tracking.
  • The result: Saving a recipe went from minutes of pausing and typing to about 4 seconds, or under a second if someone already imported that link. Morsel is live on Android with a paid plan on sale.
  • Still to do: The iPhone version is in testing ahead of its App Store release, and usage tracking isn’t in place yet, so there are no usage numbers for the newer features.

Tools: Expo (React Native), TypeScript, expo-sqlite, Node backend, Supabase, RevenueCat, Gemini, Apify, USDA FoodData Central, Open Food Facts

Status: Version 1.0.22. Free tier plus a Morsel Plus subscription, which has been on sale on Android since 1.0.21.

Overview

Morsel started with one job: paste a recipe link from Instagram, TikTok or a blog, and get back a clean recipe card that works offline. That first version shipped in July 2026.

Over the next three months it grew into a cooking companion. It now has a community feed for finding recipes, a meal planner that writes your grocery list, calorie and water tracking, home-screen widgets, and optional sync between devices. This case study covers the original problem of saving recipes, then the decisions that let the product grow without losing what made it work: it stays cheap to run, it never forces an account, and it never advertises a feature that doesn’t work yet.

Problem

Good recipes live in the worst possible formats: a 30-second Instagram reel with ingredients flashed on screen for two seconds, a TikTok voiceover you replay three times, or a blog post buried under a life story and ad banners. There’s no reliable way to save what you found, so people screenshot, bookmark tabs they never reopen, or try to remember it while cooking. Nutrition information is rarely included at all. The result is a recipe collection scattered across camera rolls, browser tabs, and saved-post folders: none of it searchable, editable, or usable without a signal mid-recipe.

Walkthrough

  • Paste a link: Instagram, TikTok, or any recipe blog or website URL, right from the Add screen.
  • Read the source: the app’s server works out where the link came from and collects the recipe. For TikTok it combines the caption with what’s said aloud, because the recipe is often only spoken. For websites it uses the site’s recipe data if there is any, and the page text if not.
  • AI extraction: AI turns that content into a structured recipe: title, servings, ingredients, steps and estimated nutrition, plus a suggested category.
  • Progress messages: “Reading the link…” → “Pulling out the recipe…” → “Estimating macros…”, so the wait doesn’t feel frozen.
  • Auto-completion: a missing photo is filled in automatically, and the recipe lands in a category (Meals, Drinks, Snacks, Desserts) or a custom folder.
  • Saved on the phone: browsing, editing and search all work without internet from this point on.
  • Cook Mode: a full-screen, one-step-at-a-time view with a progress bar and a pop-up ingredient list that adjusts amounts to your serving size.
  • Manual entry: recipes with no link can be typed in by hand. These don’t use AI or count toward any limit.

After the recipe is saved

  • Cook: Cook Mode, a serving scaler and metric/imperial conversion, all offline.
  • Plan: the Smart Planner builds a week of meals from your saved recipes around your calorie goal (Plus).
  • Shop: the plan turns into a grocery list grouped by aisle, which you can send to iOS Reminders or Android Calendar (Plus).
  • Track: calorie and water trackers built around meals and nutrition, with home-screen widgets on iOS and Android (Plus).
  • Discover: browse recipes other cooks shared, or search every recipe Morsel has already extracted. Saving from Discover is free.

How the product grew

Each month added one layer on top of the same core flow.

  • July 2026: Android launch. Link extraction, offline cookbook, Cook Mode. Free only.
  • August: Morsel Plus went on sale on Google Play (monthly and yearly). Calorie and water trackers, detailed nutrition data, Adjust to Taste, optional Google sign-in and a Spanish translation followed.
  • September: A brand refresh with a mascot, animated onboarding and new store art.
  • October: An iOS design pass and App Store review prep, the Discover feed, the Smart Planner and grocery lists, home-screen widgets, sync across devices, and a light redesign of every screen.

Key design decisions

1. Never pay twice for the same recipe

  • When someone imports a recipe, the result is saved for everyone. The first import costs money to process. Anyone who pastes that same link later gets it instantly, for free, and it doesn’t count against their monthly limit.
  • Instagram and TikTok add different tracking text to almost every shared link, so the same post can look like a different link each time. Morsel recognises the post itself, not the text of the link. Otherwise this saving would rarely happen.
  • Measured result: a new link takes about 4.3 seconds, and a link someone already imported takes about 0.3 seconds.

2. Use AI only when nothing else will do

Once Discover, content checks and tagging arrived, AI costs could have grown with every feature. I set one rule instead: AI is the last resort.

  • Every AI answer is saved and reused when the same question comes up again: content checks, recipe tags, Adjust to Taste results and “not a recipe” failures.
  • A blog that already publishes complete recipe data (steps and all four main nutrition values) is saved with no AI at all.
  • Arithmetic isn’t a job for AI. “High protein” and “low carb” are worked out from the nutrition numbers, and cook time comes from the page when the page has it.
  • Discover filters were cut down to the ones that need no extra AI: category, vegetarian, vegan, high protein, low carb, a calorie ceiling and under 30 minutes. Difficulty needs AI, and guessing “nut-free” is a risk I didn’t want anyone to take, so both were dropped.
  • Content checks run on a lighter AI with its own allowance, so posting to the community never eats into recipe imports.

3. No sign-up for the core job, optional accounts for the rest

  • Importing a recipe still needs no account. To prevent abuse and control costs, each request passes four checks: is it really from the app, is one connection sending too many requests, has this device reached its monthly limit (30 free, 200 with Plus), and has the whole service reached its daily limit.
  • The limit stays with the device even after reinstalling, and Android requests are now verified through Google Play, which closes the “reinstall to reset my limit” trick.
  • Accounts arrived later, and only for the things that need them: carrying Plus to a new phone and syncing recipes, folders and photos. Buying Plus works without signing in.

4. A community, not a social network

  • Discover shows display names, but there are no follows, profiles or per-person pages.
  • Posting needs no account either. If you do sign in, your blocks move to the account, so an unblock on one phone holds on all of them.
  • Preview, then save. Search and preview never show the full steps. The full recipe only comes with a counted save, which stops people copying the whole shared library. Saves are free and don’t count against the import limit.
  • A remix is a new post linked to the original, and a suggestion is a comment the author can mark as applied. Nobody edits someone else’s recipe. Report and block are built in.

5. One design for iPhone and Android

  • Before the iPhone release, the whole app was checked against Apple’s design guidelines: smooth rounded corners, buttons big enough to tap easily, and a floating menu bar with content scrolling behind it.
  • The tab bar is navigation only (Recipes and Profile). Adding a recipe moved to a floating button that opens a sheet.
  • Secondary controls sit on a soft fill instead of outlines, and selected states keep the brand colour.
  • The October redesign moved the background from cream to near-white and swapped coloured glows for plain neutral shadows, because the glows read as an “AI halo”. Dark mode uses pastel brand colours with dark labels on filled buttons.

6. Drawing the free / Plus line

  • Free covers the core job: 30 AI imports a month, unlimited manual recipes, folders, Cook Mode, offline access and free saves from Discover.
  • Plus raises the cap to 200 and adds planning, grocery lists, trackers, micronutrients and sync.
  • The launch rule still holds: a feature goes on the paywall only once it works. Sync showed as “coming soon” in the plan comparison until it shipped.

Impact

  • Minutes to seconds: saving a recipe takes about 4.3 seconds for a new link and about 0.3 seconds for one someone else already imported (measured at launch, July 2026).
  • A small budget that keeps working: saved answers and simple rules mean the app pays once per unique recipe, not once per user.
  • One feature to a full loop: from a single save flow in July to save, cook, plan, shop and track by October, designed and shipped by one person across 68 code updates.
  • No silent data loss: recipe photos stay small enough for Android’s automatic backup (25 MB limit), and exported backup files now include the images.
  • No sign-up wall: nothing stands between someone and a recipe they want to save, even after accounts and a community were added.

Launch numbers come from Morsel’s backend logs and vendor pricing in July 2026. The app doesn’t have usage tracking yet, so there are no usage numbers for the newer features.

What’s next

  • Release the iOS app on the App Store.
  • Add tracking for why people upgrade and where they drop off. Google Play and RevenueCat show installs and purchases today, but not the reasons.
